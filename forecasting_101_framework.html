Presentation framework · forecasting fundamentals

Points, Bands, Baselines

A session for the people across the org who already produce something they call a forecast, but have never been shown how to tell whether it is any good. Assumes spreadsheet fluency and no statistics.

GOAL 1Always ship a baseline beside the forecast.
GOAL 2Always ship an interval, not just one number.
GOAL 3Always report bias and error separately.
GOAL 4Always score on a held-out backtest.

Structure

The spine

Seven beats. If you remember only these, you can rebuild the deck from scratch. Each slide below hangs off one of them.

  1. A forecast is a falsifiable statement about the future — and most things called forecasts in the org are targets, not forecasts.
  2. One number is not a forecast. Demand is a distribution; a point forecast throws away the part the business actually needs.
  3. Error metrics make “is this good?” answerable. Bias and accuracy are different questions.
  4. “Good” only means something relative to a baseline. The baseline is not optional.
  5. The baseline is embarrassingly strong: last year, same day. Beating it is the bar, not a formality.
  6. You only learn any of this from a backtest — and backtests are easy to fool yourself with.
  7. Here is the minimum standard for a forecast readout, on one page.

How to read a slide entry

Every slide below has four parts: Message (the single sentence the audience should leave with — if there are two, split the slide), Show (one chart or one table, no more), Say (talking points in order), and Watch for (the objection that surfaces right there). Section 1

What a forecast actually is

Three slides. The job here is definitional, and it is where most of the room's real problem lives — they have never separated the forecast from the target.

01

Three things people call a forecast

Core

A forecast, a target, and a plan are three different objects. Mixing them is the single most common failure in the room.

Show

ForecastTargetPlan
Question it answersWhat do we expect?What do we want?What will we do?
OwnerAnalystLeadershipOperator
Can it be wrong?Yes, measurablyNo — it's a choiceNo — it's a commitment
Should it be unbiased?YesN/AN/A

Say

  • Only the first column is scored for accuracy. The other two are decisions.
  • If one number does two of these jobs, it gets pulled toward the incentive and away from the truth — and it will be biased in a consistent direction.
  • Ask the room: “when you produce your number, which column is it in?” Most will answer all three.

Watch for

“But leadership wants one number.” They can have one — a deliberately chosen quantile of the forecast (slide 6). That is different from bending the expectation to match the ask.

02

The three properties every forecast must state

A number without horizon, grain, and origin cannot be scored — so it cannot be improved.

Show

  • Horizon — how far ahead. Seven days out and 180 days out are different problems with different achievable accuracy. One “accuracy” number spanning mixed horizons is meaningless.
  • Grain — the time bucket and the entity level. Daily by item is a different problem than monthly by channel, and error at one grain says little about the other.
  • Origin — the as-of date. What did you know when you made it? Everything after the origin must be hidden from you.

Say

  • Origin is the one people skip, and it invalidates the most work.
  • If you built a “forecast” for March using data through April, you built a fit, not a forecast.

Watch for

“We refresh weekly, so the origin moves.” Then you have a series of forecasts, each with its own origin, and you score each at a fixed horizon from its own origin.

03

Why forecasting is hard, in one picture

Core

A series is signal — level, trend, seasonality, known events — plus noise you cannot predict. The goal is to capture all the signal and none of the noise.

Show

One real series the audience recognizes, decomposed into four stacked panels: level, weekly seasonality, annual seasonality, residual.

Say

  • Level is where the series sits; trend is which way it drifts.
  • Seasonality is usually two cycles at once — day-of-week and time-of-year. Both matter, and they interact.
  • Known events (holidays, promos, openings) are signal you can know in advance.
  • What's left is noise. A model that “fits” noise looks brilliant on history and fails forward. That's overfitting, and avoiding it is the rest of this talk.

Watch for

“Our business is unpredictable.” Partly true — which is exactly why section 2 exists. Quantifying how unpredictable is the job, not an excuse to skip it.

Section 2

Why point forecasts alone are bad

The heart of the deck. Seven slides that move from “you deleted information” to “the correct decision is now unavailable” to “here is how to fix it this week.”

04

The number you deliver summarizes something you never showed

Core

Reality is a distribution of possible outcomes. A point forecast is one summary statistic of that distribution — and you almost certainly never chose which one.

Show

A distribution curve for a single future day, with the point forecast as one tick on the x-axis and the rest of the curve shaded behind it.

Say

  • When you say “we'll sell 1,000,” you mean something closer to “the middle of a range from 700 to 1,400.” The range is real information and you deleted it.
  • The receiver assumes 1,000 is what will happen. It won't. The probability of hitting any exact point is essentially zero.
  • The honest object: p50 = 1,000, and 80% of outcomes fall between 800 and 1,250.
05

Which point is the point?

“The forecast” is ambiguous even as a single number — mean and median differ, and your error metric silently picks one for you.

Show

A right-skewed demand distribution with the mean and the median marked apart.

Say

  • Minimizing squared error targets the mean. Minimizing absolute error targets the median.
  • Demand is usually right-skewed — a floor at zero, occasional large spikes — so the mean sits above the median.
  • Two teams can therefore both be “right” and disagree materially, because they optimized different things without knowing it.

Watch for

This is the first moment people realize their metric choice is a modeling choice. Let it land — it sets up section 3.

06

No decision actually wants the middle

Core

Every real decision downstream of a forecast wants a quantile, not the average — and usually not the middle one.

Show

DecisionCost of underCost of overWhat it needs
Inventory / safety stockLost sale, lost customerCarrying cost, markdownHigh quantile (p90+)
Staffing a shiftService collapseIdle labor hoursHigh-ish quantile
Cash / commitmentBreach, emergency spendIdle capitalDepends — but stated
Capacity investmentCan't serve demandStranded assetFull distribution

Say

  • If running out costs four times what holding extra costs, you should deliberately plan above your expected value. That isn't optimism, it's arithmetic.
  • You cannot do that arithmetic with a point forecast — the required information, how wide the uncertainty is, was discarded before the decision-maker saw it.
  • This is the strongest argument in the deck. Point forecasts don't merely lose precision; they make the correct decision unavailable.
07

You can't tell a bad forecast from an unlucky one

Without an interval, “we missed by 12%” has no interpretation.

Say

  • Forecast said 1,000; actual was 1,200. Broken model, or a normal Tuesday?
  • With a band the question is answerable. If the 80% band was 950–1,050, the model is broken. If it was 800–1,250, that was a normal outcome and nothing needs fixing.
  • Without the band, every miss triggers the same response: a meeting, a manual override, “the model doesn't understand our business.” Most of those meetings are about noise.

Watch for

This slide converts the skeptics who don't care about statistics but do hate wasted meetings. Aim it at them.

08

Manual overrides without a band are unmeasurable

Core

If you adjust a forecast you need to know whether the adjustment was bigger than the noise. Otherwise you're adding variance and calling it judgment.

Say

  • Overrides are often right — a planner knows about a promo the model doesn't.
  • But a +3% override on a series whose 80% band is ±20% is indistinguishable from doing nothing, and it costs you the audit trail.
  • Minimum standard: log the pre-override and post-override forecast and score both. Within a year you'll know exactly which overrides earn their keep.
09

Intervals are cheap — you already have what you need

You don't need a sophisticated model to produce an interval. You need your own past errors.

Show

1.  Backtest your existing forecast over the last N origins.
    You now have a pile of errors:  error = forecast - actual

2.  Take the 10th and 90th percentile of those errors,
    separately for each horizon step.

3.  Your 80% band is:
        lower = forecast + p10(error)
        upper = forecast + p90(error)

Say

  • These are empirical residual quantiles. Ten lines of code, and they work on any forecast — including a spreadsheet one.
  • Then measure coverage: across the backtest, what fraction of actuals landed inside the 80% band? It should be about 80%. If it's 60%, your bands are too narrow and you are overconfident.
  • In our own bakeoff, weekly 80% bands measured 0.68–0.77 coverage — genuinely undercovering, and we report it that way. Measuring it is the win; a wrong band you know about beats a point forecast you don't.

Watch for

“Our errors aren't normally distributed.” Correct — which is exactly why we take empirical quantiles of observed errors instead of a formula with a standard deviation in it.

10

Two rules about intervals people get wrong

Core

Bands don't add, and uncertainty isn't uniform across the year.

Say

  • Do not sum daily bands into a monthly band. Adding up the daily lower bounds produces a month that essentially cannot happen — every single day would have to hit its floor simultaneously. If you need a monthly band, build it at monthly grain.
  • Uncertainty concentrates. In our series, November–December residuals run two to three times the rest of the year. One flat band is too wide in June and too narrow in December — precisely backwards from what planning needs.
  • So: report bands by horizon step, and know your high-variance windows.

Section 3

Error metrics, from scratch

Eight slides. Build from one definition. The payload is slides 13 and 14 — the bias diagnosis, then the free fix. Given that 92% of this org's forecasts land above actuals, this section is the reason to hold the session at all.

11

The one definition everything is built on

error = forecast − actual. State your sign convention out loud, every time.

Say

  • Positive error means you forecast too high. Negative means too low.
  • Half of all arguments about “is our bias positive or negative” are two people using opposite conventions. Pick one, print it on the report, never flip it.
  • Live example worth naming: our reporting calls this bias negative, because it computes actual − forecast. The underlying fact is that forecasts sit above actuals. Anyone hearing “negative bias” without the convention will go hunting for the opposite problem.
  • The rest of this section is just different ways of adding these errors up.
12

Bias: are you consistently wrong in one direction?

Core

Bias measures direction, not size. Persistent one-sided bias is a process defect, not bad luck — and it is the cheapest thing in this deck to fix.

Show

bias%  =  sum(forecast - actual)  /  sum(actual)

Say

  • Errors should cancel over a full seasonal cycle. Bias near zero means the highs and lows balance.
  • A well-centered forecast lands above the actual about half the time. Ours landed above 92% of the time over the last year. That is not a run of bad luck — coin flips don't do that. Something in the process pushes every number up.
  • Bias is not accuracy. You can be 20% off daily with zero bias (half high, half low), or 3% off daily with −3% bias (always low). These need completely different fixes.
  • Report both, always. Every forecast readout in this org should show bias and error side by side.
13

Common causes of persistent one-sided bias

Core

If your bias is always the same sign, the cause is almost always on this list — and most of them are organizational, not statistical.

Show

Read this table against the fact, not the sign: our forecasts land above actuals. Start at the top block.

SymptomLikely causeFix
OverThe forecast is the plan or budget, and the plan assumes growthSeparate forecast from target; score them separately
OverA growth factor applied to a level that is flat or decliningDrop the uplift; test replay-last-year-unscaled as the baseline
OverPlanned initiatives added at full projected value, none discounted for the ones that won't landApply a realization rate learned from past initiatives
OverAnchored on an inflated base year (a promo year, a one-off spike)Re-base on a normal year, or model the spike as an event
OverBottom-up sum of individually optimistic regional or category plansReconcile to a top-down total; check bias at the total
OverExtrapolating a trend that has already flattened or turnedDamp the trend, or drop it at long horizons
UnderOptimizing MAPE — it structurally rewards forecasting low (slide 16)Report WAPE alongside it
Under“Outlier” cleaning removes spikes but never dipsSymmetric rules only, or don't clean at all
UnderSales used as demand during stockouts (censored demand)Flag stockout periods; don't train on them
DriftingA level shift the process hasn't absorbedMonitor bias over time; alarm on sustained drift

Say

  • Notice that nearly every over-forecasting cause is a process problem wearing a math costume. None of them are fixed by a better algorithm.
  • The first two rows are the ones to press on. If the forecast doubles as the plan, it inherits the plan's growth assumption — and if the underlying level is flat or drifting down, that assumption is wrong in the same direction every single period.
  • Fixing bias is nearly free and usually worth more than a better model, because it's systematic — it compounds across every row.

Watch for

This is the slide the audience recognizes itself in, and the room may get defensive. Keep it structural: nobody chose to over-forecast, the process chose for them. Confirm the 92% figure yourself before presenting it.

14

The 92% problem: subtract the offset before you buy a model

Core

When 92% of your forecasts land above actuals, most of your error isn't noise — it's a fixed offset. Subtracting it is arithmetic, and it is free.

Show

Share of periods where forecast > actual, over one year

    a well-centered forecast     ~50%
    ours                          92%

The fix, in one line:

    debiased = forecast - (median error over a trailing window)

Say

  • Split your error into two parts: a systematic part that's there every period, and a random part that isn't. Only the random part is genuinely hard.
  • 92% one-sided tells you the split. Under a normal-error assumption it implies the offset is about 1.4× the size of the random noise — the systematic part dominates. Removing it alone would cut average absolute error by roughly 40–45%.
  • Treat that number as an order-of-magnitude estimate, not a promise: it assumes the offset is stable and the errors are roughly symmetric around it. Measure the real figure on your own backtest — it takes an afternoon and it is the highest-return afternoon available to this team.
  • This is also why a one-line seasonal naive is beating us by 20–30%. It isn't smarter. It simply has no growth assumption baked in, so it doesn't carry the offset.
  • Do it honestly: estimate the offset on a trailing window of past origins only, never on the window you're scoring, and re-check it quarterly. A bias correction is itself a forecast, and it goes stale.
  • Correct only what's stable. If the offset drifts period to period, subtracting a constant will overshoot — go fix the cause on slide 13 instead.

Watch for

“That's just fudging the number.” The opposite: a persistent one-sided error is free information you are currently throwing away every period. Ignoring it is the fudge. The real objection to surface is whether the offset exists because the forecast is doing a second job as the plan — if so, this slide is a patch and slide 13 is the cure.

15

Accuracy: MAE and RMSE

MAE is the typical miss in units. RMSE punishes big misses much harder. Neither compares across series.

Show

MAE   =  mean( |forecast - actual| )             "average miss, in units"
RMSE  =  sqrt( mean( (forecast - actual)^2 ) )   "big misses weighted heavier"

Say

  • Use MAE when a 10-unit miss is exactly twice as bad as a 5-unit miss.
  • Use RMSE when one enormous miss is disproportionately damaging — capacity breaks, service failures.
  • Both are in units, so they read naturally, but they can't be compared across items of different size. An MAE of 500 is superb on one series and catastrophic on another.
16

WAPE — and why not MAPE

Core

WAPE is the default percentage-error metric. MAPE is the trap, and it may be the reason your bias is negative.

Show

WAPE  =  sum( |forecast - actual| )  /  sum( actual )      <- use this
MAPE  =  mean( |forecast - actual|   /       actual  )      <- avoid

Say

  • WAPE is total absolute error as a share of total volume. It's volume-weighted, so the big days count more — which is what the business cares about.
  • MAPE fails three ways. One: undefined at zero, and it explodes for small actuals, so a single near-zero day can dominate. Two: it's asymmetric — under-forecasting caps out at 100% error, while over-forecasting is punished without limit. Optimizing MAPE literally trains you to forecast low. Three: it's unweighted, so a 100% error on a 3-unit day counts the same as a 5% error on a 10,000-unit day.
  • Note the direction carefully here, because it's diagnostic. MAPE's asymmetry pushes forecasts down. Ours run high anyway. So whatever is lifting our numbers is strong enough to overpower the metric that's pushing back — which points squarely at the organizational causes on slide 13, not at a technique problem.

Watch for

MAPE is entrenched everywhere. Don't fight it head-on — just put WAPE next to it in every report and let people watch the two disagree.

17

Metric hygiene

Most metric disputes are about which rows were scored, not which formula was used.

Say

  • Never average percentages across items. Aggregate the numerator and the denominator, then divide.
  • Score at the grain where the decision is made. A forecast can be excellent monthly and useless daily — errors cancel as you aggregate up.
  • Compare models only on identical rows: same dates, same items, same horizons.
  • Exclude nothing after seeing results. If you drop a bad window, disclose it and say why.
  • If you filled missing dates, say how many and treat the metric as approximate.
18

For probabilistic forecasts: coverage and width

Core

Once you ship intervals you need two more numbers to know whether they're honest.

Say

  • Coverage — the share of actuals that fell inside the band. An 80% band should catch about 80%. Below that, you're overconfident; far above, the band is uselessly wide.
  • Average width — how wide the band is. Coverage alone is gameable: a band from zero to infinity has perfect coverage and zero value. Always report width beside it.
  • For the analytically minded, mention pinball loss — it scores the whole distribution in one number. Mention it, don't teach it. Coverage plus width covers 90% of the need.

Section 4

The baseline rule

Four slides, and the one the room will remember. Deliver it without smugness — some of the audience built the forecast that's about to lose.

19

“Is 8% WAPE good?”

Unanswerable in isolation. Accuracy only means something relative to a baseline scored on the same rows.

Say

  • 8% WAPE on a stable aggregate might be terrible. 20% WAPE on a volatile sparse item might be excellent.
  • The only sound question: did it beat a dumb rule?
20

The baseline: seasonal naive

Core

“Same period last year” is not a strawman. It is a genuinely strong forecast, and it is the bar.

Show

lag 364:  forecast(today) = actual(364 days ago)   -> keeps the SAME WEEKDAY
lag 365:  forecast(today) = actual(365 days ago)   -> keeps the SAME CALENDAR DATE

Say

  • 364 is exactly 52 weeks, so Saturday maps to Saturday. Use it when day-of-week drives the series.
  • 365 maps calendar date to calendar date. Use it when fixed-date events — Christmas, July 4th — dominate.
  • You have to choose, and the choice is worth real accuracy. In our work the daily champion uses lag 364, while the weekly champion forecasts daily at lag 365 and sums into weeks — because weekly buckets misallocate the pre-Christmas surge when the holiday weekday shifts year to year. That single change roughly halved the error on the December fold (10.5% → 5.0% WAPE).
  • Why it beats us specifically: replaying last year unscaled carries no growth assumption. When the underlying level is flat or drifting down, that makes it close to bias-centered by construction — while any process that starts from a plan number inherits the plan's optimism every period.
  • It costs one line of SQL. There is no excuse for not having it beside every forecast.
21

Skill: the only number that says “worth it”

Report improvement over the baseline, as a percentage, on identical rows.

Show

skill  =  1  -  ( WAPE_yours / WAPE_baseline )

Say

  • +20% skill: your forecast cuts error by a fifth versus doing nothing clever. Real value.
  • 0%: you spent a quarter matching a one-line rule.
  • Negative: the baseline is better and should replace your forecast today.
  • Make this a required field on every readout. It ends the “our model is sophisticated” conversation immediately and replaces it with a number.
22

What we learned running the bakeoff properly

We tested six-plus model families against seasonal naive on honest backtests. The naive baseline won essentially everywhere.

Show — what lost

  • Gradient-boosted trees (HistGradientBoosting, Extra Trees, Random Forest, LightGBM)
  • Ridge regression on lag features
  • Classical statistical models (ARIMA, ETS, SARIMAX, MSTL)
  • Foundation models (TimesFM, Chronos-Bolt, Chronos-2)
  • Learned ensembles and stacks (convex weights, inverse-error weights)
  • Hand-designed seasonal variants (level-scaled, multi-year averaged, holiday-aligned)

Say

  • This is not “models are useless.” It's that all of these re-model the same sales history. Once level and seasonality are captured there is nothing left to squeeze without new information — covariates like a promo calendar, planned openings, price — or finer structure.
  • At sub-aggregate levels the picture flips: per-market and per-channel models did beat naive. Complexity pays where the series is noisier and the baseline is weaker.
  • Takeaway for the room: start at the baseline, then justify every step away from it.

Watch for

“So we shouldn't use models?” No — you should earn them. A bakeoff is how you find out, and it takes hours, not months.

Section 5

Backtesting: how you actually know

Four slides. Slide 24 is the credibility slide — tell the story where we were the ones fooled, and you earn the right to critique their work in section 7.

23

The only honest test

Core

Pick a cutoff. Hide everything after it. Forecast. Compare. Roll forward. Repeat.

Show

A timeline: training window, cutoff line, forecast horizon, held-out actuals. Then the same picture stepped forward three times.

Say

  • One cutoff is an anecdote. You need several, at the horizon you actually use.
  • Score at the same horizon each time so you're measuring one thing.
  • That's the whole method. Everything after this slide is about how it goes wrong.
24

Leakage: the mistake that produced our own fake winner

If any information from after the cutoff touched the forecast, the score is fiction — and it will be flatteringly wrong.

Say

  • Real example from this project: we built an ensemble whose blend weights were fitted on a window that overlapped the evaluation window — by 31 days at the 91-day horizon, and by roughly 11 months at the 365-day horizon.
  • The ensemble looked like a clear winner and was on track to ship.
  • When the weights were refit properly inside each fold — using only data ending at or before that fold's cutoff — the ensemble lost every single fold at every horizon to plain seasonal naive.
  • The root cause was mundane: the weights were chasing which model used to be better, on a window it had already seen.
  • Name the common leaks: fitting anything on post-cutoff data (weights, parameters, scalers, feature selection, even “which model to use”); using a field that isn't knowable at forecast time; cleaning outliers using the full history.

Watch for

Nobody argues with this slide, because you're the one confessing. That's the point — it buys the authority you need for the workshop.

25

Overlapping folds: “five tests” that are really one

If your test windows overlap, you have far fewer independent reads than you think.

Say

  • Five folds of a 52-week forecast stepped four weeks apart share about 48 of 52 weeks with their neighbor. That's roughly one effective independent window — one opinion, quoted five times.
  • We compute and print “effective independent windows” on every run for exactly this reason. At 26- and 52-week horizons it comes out around 1.1–1.6.
  • Practical rule: at long horizons you simply do not have much evidence. Say so out loud instead of reporting “won 4 of 5 folds” as though it were strong.
26

Don't fool yourself: pre-registration and tiny deltas

If you try forty things, one wins by chance. Decide what counts as a win before you look.

Say

  • Write down the candidate, the baseline, the horizons, the folds, and the threshold that would make you switch — before scoring. We keep a pre-registration log with the verdict appended after. Most entries read “rejected,” and that's a healthy log.
  • A challenger beating the champion by under ~2% relative on overlapping folds is a tie, not a win. Eyeballing “4 of 5 folds” is not evidence.
  • To promote on a small margin, test the per-window differences properly, with overlap-aware error bars. Otherwise keep the incumbent.
  • Corollary: never compare a model scored on one fold design against a model scored on another. Rebuild both on the same folds or don't compare them.

28

Maturity ladder: where is your forecast today?

Nobody is expected at level 3 tomorrow. Everyone should know their level and their next step.

Show

LevelWhat you haveNext step
0A number from judgment or a spreadsheet trend. No score.Backtest it once. Get a WAPE.
1Backtested point forecast with WAPE and bias.Add a seasonal naive baseline; report skill.
2Beats baseline, reports bias, ships p10/p50/p90.Measure coverage. Fix it if it isn't ~80%.
3Calibrated intervals, monitoring, a tracked challenger.Add new information (covariates) — not new models.

Say

  • Level 0 → 1 is the single biggest jump in value in this deck, and it takes an afternoon.
  • Note where level 3 points: once you're past the baseline, the lever is new data, not fancier algorithms. That's the durable lesson from our bakeoff.
29

What to do Monday

Three concrete actions, in order, with honest time estimates.

Say

  1. Compute your baseline. Pull last year's same-weekday actuals for the period you forecast. Score your forecast and the baseline on the same rows. Report skill. (One afternoon.)
  2. Count your one-sided rate. Over the last 12 months, in what share of periods was your forecast above the actual? If it isn't near 50%, subtract the trailing median error and re-score — then walk the slide 13 table to find out why the offset is there. (One hour for the count, an afternoon for the re-score.)
  3. Build a band from your own backtest errors — p10/p90 by horizon step — and start reporting three numbers instead of one. Measure coverage next quarter. (One day.)

Close with the offer: bring your forecast file to the follow-up and we'll do step 1 together.

Section 7

Workshop material

Not slides. This is the facilitator's kit — the live demo, the objections you will get, the vocabulary, and the prep list.

Live demo script — 10 minutes, do it live if you can

Nothing lands like watching a one-line rule beat a forecast someone defended in a meeting.

  1. Take a real forecast the audience produced, with its origin date and horizon.
  2. Pull the actuals for that window, and the actuals from 364 days before each date.
  3. On the same rows, compute WAPE and bias for their forecast and for the naive.
  4. Show skill: 1 − WAPE_theirs / WAPE_naive.
  5. Show the third line: their forecast minus its own trailing median error. This is usually the moment the room goes quiet — a forecast nobody re-modelled, just re-centred, that beats the one they defended last quarter.
  6. Then take p10/p90 of their errors and show what their 80% band would have been.

Handle the result carefully either way. If naive wins, frame it as “this is the bar, and there's a free 20–30% improvement available to you today” — not as an indictment; some of the audience built that forecast. If their forecast wins, even better: they now have a defensible claim they never had before, and the readout template lets them keep proving it.

Objection handling

ObjectionResponse
“Our business is too volatile to forecast.”Then quantify the volatility and ship a wide interval. That is the forecast. Volatility is a finding, not a blocker.
“Naive can't know about our strategy or promos.”Correct — and it still beats us, which means our strategy knowledge isn't reaching the number. That's a fixable gap, and the highest-value one.
“Leadership only wants one number.”They get one: a deliberately chosen quantile. The band goes in the appendix and drives the safety-stock decision.
“We don't have time to backtest.”Baseline plus backtest is an afternoon — cheaper than one meeting arguing about whether the forecast is good.
“Our accuracy is 92%.”92% by which metric, on which rows, at which horizon, versus which baseline? Usually this is 100 − MAPE at a grain where errors cancel.
“We already adjust for that manually.”Good — log the pre- and post-override numbers and let's score the overrides. If they add skill, we'll build them in.

GrainTime bucket plus entity level.
BaselineThe dumb rule you must beat. Default: seasonal naive.
SkillPercent error reduction versus the baseline.
BiasSigned average error — the direction of systematic wrongness.
WAPETotal absolute error ÷ total actual.

LeakageAny post-cutoff information influencing the forecast.
OverfittingFitting noise — looks great on history, fails forward.
